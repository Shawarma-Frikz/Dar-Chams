Dar Chams

A virtual house in Tunis that lives on solar power. It forecasts the sun for the next two days, turns its panels toward it, watches the battery, and decides which appliance gets electricity when there is not enough for everyone. Everything runs as a simulation, on free tools, with a dashboard that works on a phone and a computer.

Part I
The project

What you are building

A small, complete system that you can run on a weak laptop. A real physical model of the sun and the panels, a battery, a house full of appliances, a decision engine, three kinds of AI, and two faces to look at it: a 2D dashboard and a 3D house.

01 · SimulateA house that behaves

Real weather for Tunis drives the sun position, panel output, panel temperature, battery charge and eleven appliances with daily habits. A virtual ESP32 sends the readings a real one would send.

02 · DecideAn energy brain

It forecasts production and use for today, tomorrow and the day after, then protects the battery by cutting appliances in priority order, and moves the panels only when the extra sun is worth the energy spent moving them.

03 · ShowTwo ways to look

A responsive dashboard installed like a phone app, and a 3D twin where the sun crosses the sky, the panels turn, and appliances light up or go dark. A chat box answers questions in plain words.

System map

Your constraints, written down

  • Simulation only. No hardware exists yet. The virtual ESP32 sends the same JSON a real one would, and a Wokwi ESP32 in the browser sends it too, so hardware can replace either later without touching the rest.
  • No local LLM. Training happens on Google Colab, inference runs as tiny models inside the app, and the chat box calls a free hosted API with a rule-based fallback.
  • Free tools only. Every tool in Part III has a free tier or is open source. Free tiers change, so the stack table says what to re-check.
  • React and Next.js. One codebase holds the dashboard, the API routes, the simulation engine and the 3D scene.
  • A weak PC. The simulation is light arithmetic. The heavy parts are 3D and model training, so 3D loads on demand with a quality switch and training stays in the cloud.
Scope boundary

Every result is simulated, and the report says so. A tracker gain or a forecast error measured here is a property of your model and the weather data, not a measurement of a real roof. Calling it a simulated result keeps it defensible.

The headline contribution

A student project is remembered for one sharp, provable idea. Yours has two that share one mechanism, a forecast that looks ahead:

  • Forecast-aware shedding. The system starts saving energy today because tomorrow will be cloudy, instead of reacting only when the battery is already low.
  • Forecast-aware tracking. The panels move only when the expected extra energy is larger than the energy the motors use.

Part IV
The virtual world

Weather, physics, battery, house

The simulation core is plain TypeScript with no framework. It takes weather and settings in, and returns power flows and states out. Because it has no screen code, it is easy to test and the same code runs in the browser, on the server and in tests.

Place and time

  • Location. Tunis, latitude 36.8065° N, longitude 10.1815° E. Make it a setting so another city works too.
  • Clock. Store every time in UTC. Tunisia is UTC+1 all year, so display time is UTC plus one hour. A single converter function does it so no other file touches time zones.
  • Step. One simulated minute per step. Weather arrives hourly, so interpolate it to minutes. Record a reading every simulated minute while you watch, and downsample to five minutes before saving long runs.
  • Seasons. At this latitude the noon sun stands 29.8° high on 21 December, 53.2° at the equinox and 76.6° on 21 June. Winter is the hard test for the battery and the priority engine. Summer is the hard test for heat and air conditioning.

  • Synthetic fallback. If a fetch fails and no fixture exists, generate a clear-sky curve scaled by a random cloud factor, and mark every chart "synthetic weather".

Sun and panel physics

Work in this order, one function per step, each with a unit test.

1. Sun position
   SunCalc.getPosition(date, lat, lon) gives altitude (radians) and azimuth (radians)
   azimuth is measured from SOUTH, positive toward WEST
   compass azimuth from north, clockwise = azimuth + PI

2. Angle between sun and panel normal (panel tilt b, compass azimuth gp)
   cos(theta) = sin(alt)*cos(b) + cos(alt)*sin(b)*cos(gs - gp)
   gs = compass azimuth of the sun

3. Light on the panel plane (plane-of-array, POA), isotropic sky model
   POA = DNI*max(0, cos(theta)) + DHI*(1 + cos(b))/2 + GHI*albedo*(1 - cos(b))/2
   albedo = 0.2

4. Cell temperature
   Tcell = Tair + (NOCT - 20)/800 * POA        NOCT = 45 C

5. DC power
   Pdc = Pstc * (POA/1000) * (1 + gamma*(Tcell - 25)) * (1 - soiling) * 0.97 * iam
   gamma = -0.004 per C,  Pstc = 3000 W,  0.97 covers wiring and mismatch
   iam = 1 - 0.05*(1/cos(theta) - 1), limited to 0..1, simple angle-of-incidence loss

6. Inverter
   Pac = min(Pdc * 0.96, 3000)

Cloudy skies make step 3 matter: DNI falls and DHI rises, so a tilted panel and a flat panel produce almost the same. That is the physical reason a tracker gains little on cloudy days.

Upgrade path

The isotropic sky model is easy to explain and good enough for a first version. If time allows, swap step 3 for the Hay-Davies model and show how much the yearly result moves. That is a nice line in the report.

Energy bookkeeping test

Every run ends with a balance: panel energy = loads served + battery change + battery losses + inverter losses + curtailed energy. The test fails if the gap exceeds 0.1 percent of production. This one test catches most bugs.

For realism, the virtual sensor also reports battery voltage from a lookup table of voltage against charge level for your chosen chemistry. The dashboard shows the percentage and energy in Wh, and the voltage appears only in the telemetry detail.

Part V
The energy brain

Priority, forecast and AI

Four decisions run every few simulated minutes: what the sun will do, which appliance gets power, whether the panels should move, and whether anything looks wrong. All four are readable rules or small models, and every decision is written to a log in plain words.

  • Hysteresis. A device that is cut at one level returns only 10 points higher, so nothing flickers on and off around a threshold.
  • Minimum times. Each device has a minimum on time and off time. The fridge compressor never restarts within three minutes.
  • Power limit. If demand exceeds what the inverter and battery can deliver together, cut the lowest tier first, and the biggest consumer first inside a tier, until demand fits.
  • Forecast shift. Every threshold above rises by a shift that grows with the forecast deficit. If the forecast says the next 24 hours will fall short of the house's need by 40 % of usable battery energy, shift every threshold up by up to 10 points. The maximum shift is a setting.
  • Deferrable scheduling. Each deferrable device has an earliest start, a deadline and a duration. The engine looks at the forecast surplus hour by hour and places each device in the best slot before its deadline, earliest deadline first. A device whose deadline arrives without a slot is forced on if the battery allows it, and the log says so.
  • Manual control. A person can pin a device on or off, with an expiry time. A pin never overrides the cutoff, because that rule protects the battery.
every step:

  shift   = clamp(forecastDeficit * maxShift, 0, maxShift)

Tracker policy

every 10 simulated minutes:
  target   = idealAngle(sun)                         // from the tracker model
  error    = angle between current pose and target
  if wind > stowLimit:   stow(); stop
  if night:              park(east); stop
  if error < deadband:   stop
  gain     = forecast(POA at target) - forecast(POA at current pose)    // next 30 min, Wh
  cost     = driveTime * movePower                    // Wh
  if gain > cost * margin:  move(target)
  else:                     log("TRACKER_HOLD", gain, cost)

On cloudy intervals the gain is tiny, so the tracker holds still and saves motor energy. Test two alternatives against it: a tracker that moves every interval, and a policy that lies flat when the diffuse share is high. Let the simulation decide which is best instead of assuming.

Forecast engine

  1. Weather forecast. Pull the hourly forecast for three days from Open-Meteo.
  2. Panel conversion. Run it through the same physics model as the simulation, which gives an hourly production estimate for the fixed panel and the tracker.
  3. Use forecast. Predict house use from the device habits plus the average of the last seven days, with a weekday and season flag.
  4. Battery roll-forward. Step the battery hour by hour through production minus use to get the battery level at the end of today, tomorrow and the day after.
  5. Uncertainty band. Run the roll-forward three times, with production at 80 %, 100 % and 120 % of the estimate, and draw the band. Replace the fixed factors with measured error percentiles once you have history.
  6. Confidence label. Today is "High", tomorrow is "Medium" and the day after is "Low" until your measured errors say otherwise.

Sentences to produce: "Today: about 6.2 kWh of surplus. The battery ends the day near 85 %. Tomorrow: cloudy, about 1.8 kWh short. The battery drops to about 30 % and may run out near 22:00. Confidence: medium."

Machine learning, trained in the cloud

A · Forecast corrector

  • Target. Simulated production divided by clear-sky production, per hour. Predict the ratio, then multiply back.
  • Features. Forecast cloud cover, forecast irradiance, hour of day, day of year, temperature, wind, last hour's measured ratio, and the ratio at the same hour yesterday.
  • Data. Open-Meteo archive for Tunis over two to three years, paired with the simulated production for the same hours. Hold out the most recent two months for testing, and split by whole days.
  • Models. Persistence baseline, raw forecast conversion, ridge regression, then LightGBM or a small neural network.
  • Metrics. Mean absolute error, root mean square error, and skill score against persistence, shown for clear and cloudy days separately.
  • Shipping. First version: export ridge weights as a JSON file and compute in TypeScript, which needs no extra package. Upgrade path: export to ONNX and run it in the browser with onnxruntime-web.

B · Anomaly watch

  • Signal. Residual = twin's expected output minus measured output, scaled by expected output.
  • Detectors. Rolling average and standard deviation for slow drift, a cumulative sum detector (CUSUM) for steps, and a time-of-day profile for repeating dips.
  • Labels. Slow steady loss that rain fixes means dust. A repeating dip at the same hour means shading. A sudden step means a fault. A loss on hot hours only means heat.
  • Truth. You inject the faults, so you know the right answer. Report recall on injected faults and false alerts per week on clean weeks.

C · Smart scheduler

The earliest-deadline-first placement above, plus an optional improvement: a small search that tries a few start times for each deferrable device and scores them on leftover energy at the end of the day. Compare it with the simple version and report if it helps.

D · Learned tracker gain (optional)

A tiny regression that predicts the next half hour's tracking gain from cloud cover, diffuse share and sun height. It replaces the physics estimate in the tracker policy only if it is as accurate and cheaper to run.

Chat assistant, safe and free

  1. The user types a question.
  2. The server builds a small facts object from your own computed numbers: current battery level, today's production, forecast, last twelve decisions, active alerts.
  3. The server sends the question and facts to the free LLM API with strict instructions: answer only from the facts, say "I do not have that" otherwise, keep it short, state units.
  4. The reply is shown. If the API fails or the free quota is used up, a rule-based answerer matches the question to a template ("battery", "tomorrow", "why was X cut", "how much did tracking gain") and fills it from the same facts.
  • The API key lives only on the server, in an environment variable. Never place it in browser code.
  • Cache identical questions for one minute and rate-limit each visitor so the free quota survives a demo.
  • Test with a fixed list of twenty questions. Every number in an answer must match the facts object, and you check that by code, not by eye.

Part VII
Virtual hardware

The Wokwi field node

Wokwi is a free simulator that runs in the browser. In it you build a virtual ESP32 board with sensors, LEDs, a display, a servo and buttons. This is the closest you can get to the real device without buying anything, and it makes the project a true IoT system: a device that measures, decides, acts, and talks to a cloud dashboard.

What the node is for

  • Measure. Sliders stand in for the panel, the battery and the wind. A light sensor, an air temperature sensor and a panel temperature sensor give the rest.
  • Decide. The firmware holds a small copy of the priority engine, so the node still protects the critical loads when the internet is gone.
  • Act. Eleven LEDs are the eleven appliances. A servo is the tracker. A buzzer is the alarm. A small screen shows the state.
  • Report. Once every few seconds it sends the telemetry JSON from Part VI to your app, and it asks the app whether there are commands for it.

The node is the "measured" side of the digital twin. The simulation core is the "expected" side. When the two disagree, the anomaly watch has something real to look at: you drag the panel slider down while the light sensor stays bright, and the app flags a panel fault.

void  drawScreen();        // SoC, power flow, shed count, link state

void  pollCommands();      // GET /api/commands?since=..., apply pin, release, tilt
void  injectFaults();      // buttons: dust lowers panelW by 15 %, fault by 33 %

unsigned long tRead = 0, tPost = 0, tPoll = 0;

void loop() {
  unsigned long now = millis();
  if (now - tRead >= 500)   { tRead = now; readSensors(); decide(); writeOutputs(); drawScreen(); }
  if (now - tPost >= 10000) { tPost = now; linkUp = postTelemetry(); }
  if (now - tPoll >= 5000)  { tPoll = now; if (linkUp) pollCommands(); }
  injectFaults();
}

The WiFi network name in Wokwi is Wokwi-GUEST with an empty password. Use the secure client for the HTTPS call and turn certificate checking off for the simulator only, and put a clear comment beside that line saying it must change on real hardware.

Sharing the brain between the app and the node

The firmware has its own copy of the priority logic, so it can run alone. To keep the two copies honest, write test vectors once as a JSON file: a list of inputs (battery percent, panel watts, load, time, device states) and the expected on and off list. The TypeScript engine runs them in Vitest. The firmware runs the same list at startup in a self-test mode and prints pass or fail to the serial monitor. If the two ever disagree, one of them has a bug, and you know which case found it.

What the node sends and receives

  • Out. The telemetry JSON from Part VI, with "source": "wokwi". The node reports one array, so make panel.fixed and panel.tracker optional in the schema and require at least one. Add an optional node object with link state, mode and uptime.
  • In. GET /api/commands?since=<id> returns a short list such as pin a device on, release it, set tilt to 40, or reset a fault. Each command has an id, so the node never applies the same one twice, and it acknowledges by sending the last applied id in its next post.
  • Store. Add a commands table: id, target, action, value, created, applied. The control route writes to it. The node reads from it.
  • Time. The node gets its clock by NTP through the same network. If that fails, the server stamps the reading when it arrives.

Safety rules for a node that talks to a cloud

  • The device key is not a secret in a public project. Anyone who opens a public Wokwi project can read the sketch. Use a throwaway key that can only write telemetry with source "wokwi", rate limit it, and rotate it after the defense. Or keep the Wokwi project private if your plan allows it.
  • Critical loads never depend on the cloud. Commands may turn a load off only if the local engine agrees it is safe, and they can never turn a Critical device off at all.
  • Fail safe. If no command or response arrives for 60 seconds, the node falls back to its local rules and lights the "no link" LED.
  • Bounds. Reject a tilt outside 0 to 80 degrees and any command id it has seen before.

Free limits and honest caveats

  • The browser simulator and a free account are enough for everything above. Check the current plan page for how many projects you can save and whether private projects are included.
  • Do not plan on the paid features: continuous integration runs, VS Code licences and private gateways. Nothing in this roadmap needs them.
  • Simulation speed depends on your computer, so a 10 second posting interval in the sketch can look slower on a weak machine. That is fine. Write real and simulated seconds apart in the report.
  • A public MQTT broker is an alternative to HTTPS if the secure call is too slow in the simulator. Public brokers are readable by anyone, so use a long random topic name and send nothing private. HTTPS to your own API stays the main route because it matches the contract.
  • Download the project files after each task and commit them to firmware/wokwi/, because the online copy is not your backup.
Claim you can make

"The same telemetry contract is produced by the physics simulation and by an ESP32 firmware running in a hardware simulator, and the dashboard cannot tell them apart except for the source label." That is true by construction, and it is the reason the contract exists.

Ethics, limits and honest wording

  • It is a simulation. Power, battery and tracker values come from models and assumptions. Do not present them as measurements of a real house.
  • Assumptions are listed. Soiling, battery aging, motor power, appliance habits and the battery voltage curve are all assumptions. Put them in one table in the report.
  • No safety claims. Nothing here is an electrical design. The roadmap never switches mains power, and any hardware step uses low-voltage direct current and a hardware cutoff.
  • Data credit. Open-Meteo asks for attribution. Keep the credit line in the footer of the app and in the report.
  • Chat limits. The assistant explains the numbers that the app computed. It does not give advice about electrical work or real purchases.
  • Privacy. There are no user accounts and no personal data. The Wokwi project and its key are treated as public.
